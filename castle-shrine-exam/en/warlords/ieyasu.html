Tokugawa Ieyasu

徳川家康 (とくがわ いえやす)

Years1543–1616
HouseTokugawa clan
RegionKanto
EraEarly Edo period

Overview

Born into a minor daimyo family of Mikawa, he passed through years as a hostage of the Imagawa and the Oda before gaining independence. Surviving the Battle of Nagashino and the Battle of Komaki-Nagakute, he won the Battle of Sekigahara, became Sei-i Taishogun (shogun), and founded the Edo shogunate. He destroyed the Toyotomi clan in the Siege of Osaka and laid the foundations of a shogunate that would last 260 years.

Life

Born as the heir of Matsudaira Hirotada, lord of Okazaki Castle, he spent his childhood in Sunpu as a hostage of Imagawa Yoshimoto. After the Battle of Okehazama he became independent of the Imagawa clan and formed an alliance with Oda Nobunaga (the Kiyosu Alliance). At the Battle of Mikatagahara (1573) he was heavily defeated by Takeda Shingen, but at the Battle of Nagashino he won fighting on the Oda side; after the Honno-ji Incident he confronted Toyotomi Hideyoshi at Komaki-Nagakute while ultimately submitting to him. In 1590 he was transferred to the Kanto region and made Edo Castle his seat. In the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 he defeated the Western Army of Ishida Mitsunari and others, and in 1603 he became shogun and founded the Edo shogunate. He destroyed the Toyotomi clan in the Siege of Osaka of 1614–15, and died in Sunpu the following year, 1616.

In battle

Ieyasu's generalship was characterized by a caution learned from defeat. Having passed through the crushing defeat at Mikatagahara and local victories at Komaki-Nagakute, he did not hurry into decisive battles between great armies; instead, a style of fighting stands out in which he shaped the situation through diplomacy and stratagem before moving. At Sekigahara, groundwork laid before the battle solidified the Eastern Army's unity, and through defections such as that of Kobayakawa Hideaki the battle was settled in half a day. Many anecdotes are also told of retainers who supported him at the risk of their lives during the retreat to Hamamatsu.

Arms and equipment

Ieyasu was fond of falconry, which he is known to have continued as a way of maintaining his health. Rather than a warrior in his own person, he was a commander who learned the ways of war of the Takeda and the Oda at close hand and applied them to the organization of his retainer band and the gathering of information. The tradition of the portrait called the 'Shikami-zo' (the 'grimacing portrait')—said to have been painted of himself after the defeat at Mikatagahara as a warning to himself—is famous, but there are various theories about its origin.

Character and records

His image as a man of patience, spoken of in the verse 'If the cuckoo will not sing, wait until it does,' is a creation of later times. On the other hand, the calculation visible in the placement of daimyo after Sekigahara and in the conduct of the Siege of Osaka can be glimpsed in the sources. The words regarded as his last instruction, 'A person's life is like traveling a long road bearing a heavy burden,' are likewise considered to have been compiled in later times.

In literature

tokugawa, sekigahara, taikoki

Tokugawa Ieyasu is an epic novel depicting Ieyasu's life. In Sekigahara, Ieyasu's figure is drawn through his confrontation with Mitsunari, and in Shinsho Taikoki through his relationship with Hideyoshi.
