Nara's Naramachi Gods in the Spotlight: Autumn Special Exhibition at Gangoji Temple Until November 15

This is a news article bringing you the latest developments concerning castles and shrines. Source: the Sankei Shimbun (October 26, 2026). At the Horin-kan hall of Gangoji Temple in Nara City, the autumn special exhibition “The Gods of Naramachi — Gangoji's Tutelary Shrines and the Deities of the Town” is being held. Exhibiting about 70 items, including deity statues and old documents, it is a special exhibition introducing the culture of faith that has lived on in the city. It runs until November 15, with opening hours from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., and viewing is included in the Gangoji admission fee (800 yen for adults, and so on).

In Naramachi there are shrines large and small, such as Tenjinsha and Goryo Shrine, the ujigami of each town, and Sudo Tenno-sha. These shrines have been known as the tutelary shrines of Gangoji, and also in connection with goryo faith and more. It can be seen that the practice of each town enshrining a deity has long breathed within the daily life of Naramachi.

For Tenjinsha, the ujigami of the towns in the eastern part of Naramachi, a Heian-period seated statue of a male deity, a seated statue of Daishogunshin and other items will be shown. For Goryo Shrine, the ujigami of the area from central Naramachi toward the southwest, records of the shosengu (formal transfer of the deity to a new shrine) carried out in the Edo period will also be exhibited. It is an exhibition that traces the form of a town's faith from both statues and records.

The Koshin faith, known for the “migawari-zaru” (substitute monkeys) seen today at the eaves of Naramachi's townhouses, is also introduced. The first Koshin statue (a statue of Shomen Kongo) to be enshrined in the town is said to have been at Reigan-in, a temple of the Jodo school, and an Edo-period standing statue of Shomen Kongo is exhibited. In addition, a standing statue of Daikokuten from Tokuyuji Temple (Muromachi period) is also shown, in an exhibition designed to let visitors learn that it has been worshipped as a traditional god of the kitchen. Mr. Hattori, chief researcher at the Gangoji Institute for Cultural Properties Research, says, “By tracing the history of the gods, you can understand the history of the city of Nara, and glimpse the lives of the people of the time.”

This connects with themes in shrine exam study. An “ujigami” is the guardian deity of a locality, enshrined jointly by the people who live in that area; the people who enshrine it are called ujiko. A “chinju” (tutelary deity) refers to a god or shrine enshrined to protect a temple, an area or the like. “Goryo faith” is known as the faith that enshrines the spirits of those who died untimely deaths and the like, in order to calm calamities. “Koshin faith” is a folk faith known for observances such as koshin-machi, staying awake through the night on the day of Koshin, and Shomen Kongo is often enshrined as its principal deity. “Shosengu” refers to the ceremony of formally transferring the sacred object to a new shrine building on occasions such as the construction or repair of shrine buildings. Each of these is a key to understanding the gods featured in the special exhibition.

The autumn special exhibition runs until November 15, and the remaining period is not long. When you visit, check the opening hours (9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.) and the admission fee in advance before you go.

A word from Suzu: Each town has its own ujigami, and at the eaves hang migawari-zaru — Naramachi is a town where, the more you walk it, the closer you feel to the gods. Viewing the statues and records at the special exhibition first and then walking the town would surely change how the scenery looks.
