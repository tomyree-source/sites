How to Read Komainu Guardian Dogs: The Open-Mouthed Agyo, the Closed-Mouthed Ungyo, and Pairs with Balls and Pups

Komainu guardian dogs sit quietly watching over worshippers at the entrance to the shrine approach and in front of the worship hall. They seem to be at every shrine, yet each pair has its own face and posture. Once you know how to look at komainu, every visit brings a new discovery.

【The Agyo and Ungyo Pair】The komainu on your right as you face them, with its mouth open, is called the 'agyo,' and the one on the left, with its mouth closed, is the 'ungyo.' The pairing of 'a' and 'un' is said to represent the beginning and the end of words — the first and last of all things. You will also often hear that komainu serve as wards against evil at the entrance to the sacred precincts.

【Where Komainu Came From】Originally, a lion and an imaginary beast called a 'komainu' (Goryeo dog) were placed as a pair on the left and right, and over time both came to be called komainu. They have a history of being placed not only at shrines but also at the imperial court and at temples. Old ones were wooden and stood inside the worship hall; later, the form of stone komainu lining the approach became widespread.

【Balls and Pups: Individual Touches】Some shrines have komainu holding a ball (mari) under a front paw, and others have a komainu accompanied by a young one — a pup. The ball is described as carrying meanings connected with wishes and protection from evil, while the pup is often introduced in connection with hopes for thriving descendants and safe child-rearing. They are also a place for stonemasons to show their skill, and you can enjoy reading the differences in 'style' between the schools of regional stonemasons.

【Foxes and Other Messengers Are Different】The foxes at an Inari shrine are a separate presence from komainu: they are regarded as the messengers (kenzoku) of Inari Okami. Their hallmark is holding a key, a ball or an ear of rice in the mouth. That each shrine has a different guardian beast is one of the pleasures of the precincts.

【Exam Study Points】This is covered in Chapter 3 of the Shrine Certification textbook. The basic fact — 'the one with its mouth open is the agyo' — appears on the exam just as it is. Learn it together with the meaning of the word 'a-un,' checking pairs at real shrines as you go.

A trip in search of your favorite komainu is also recommended. Older shrines tend to keep more individual komainu, so on your next visit, be sure to look at what is sitting at your feet. The recommended books below are about castles, but the joy of visiting old buildings is the same.
