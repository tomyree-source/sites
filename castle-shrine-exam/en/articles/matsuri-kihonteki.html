Shrine Festival Basics: Reisai, Mikoshi and Dashi Floats Explained

When people think of a shrine festival, many picture the calls of mikoshi bearers and rows of food stalls. But a festival is not mere festivity: it is the heart of the annual events, conducted according to ritual procedure. This article organizes the basics of festivals — knowledge that also helps with exam study.

The reisai, the most important festival of the year — The reisai (reitaisai), held on the day most closely connected with the shrine, is regarded as the most important of the year's ceremonies. The day chosen is often one tied to the enshrined deity, or to the shrine's history, such as its founding or the transfer of the deity; in most cases, this reisai is what people mean by 'that shrine's festival.' On the exam, too, 'the most important ceremony is the reisai' is a standard point.

The meaning of the mikoshi and the Shinko-sai — A mikoshi (portable shrine) is the vehicle into which the spirit of the deity is transferred and carried during a festival. The event in which the mikoshi tours the parish district is called the 'Shinko-sai' (togyo, the divine procession). The deity goes out into the town and directly watches over the lives of the parishioners — this meaning lies at the center of the festival's splendor. A key point of Chapter 4 of the Shrine Exam textbook.

Dashi and yamaboko processions — In some regions, dashi floats or yamaboko parade alongside the mikoshi. Kyoto's Gion Festival float procession is known for its mountains and halberds, adorned with gorgeous hangings, advancing along the city's great avenues. The difference can be organized like this: a mikoshi 'advances bearing the deity,' while dashi and yamaboko have the stronger role of coloring the festival with music and figure decorations.

The three great festivals and the diversity of matsuri — Japan's three great festivals are usually named as Kyoto's Gion Matsuri, Osaka's Tenjin Matsuri and Tokyo's Kanda Matsuri. After a festival comes the 'naorai,' a feast bearing the meaning of deity and people sharing a meal — the way ritual and banquet form a single sequence is another important structure of the festival. Festivals tied to rice farming, such as the Kinen-sai, which prays for a rich harvest, and the Niiname-sai, which gives thanks for it, also form the backbone of the annual cycle.

Exam study points — A central theme of Chapter 4 of the Shrine Exam textbook. Be sure to fix three points: 'the reisai is the most important ceremony,' 'the mikoshi procession is the Shinko-sai (togyo),' and 'the three great festivals are the Gion, Tenjin and Kanda festivals.'

At the next festival you attend, pay attention to the mikoshi's procession route and to the neighborhoods of its bearers. You will notice that the festival's map is exactly the map of that land's parish districts.
