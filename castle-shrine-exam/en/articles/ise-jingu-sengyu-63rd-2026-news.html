A 1,300-Year Cycle of Rebuilding Every 20 Years — Ise Jingu Begins Cutting Timber for Its 63rd Shikinen Sengu

This is a news article bringing you the latest developments concerning castles and shrines. Source: The Associated Press (November 15, 2026). In the forests of the Kiso mountains — the Misoma-yama, sometimes called the Japanese Alps — priests in ceremonial white put their axes to a cypress some 300 years old. As the great tree fell to the cry of 'the tree is falling,' a deep sound echoed through the forest. The Shikinen Sengu, in which Ise Jingu rebuilds all of its shrine buildings once every 20 years, has begun cutting the sacred timber for its 63rd cycle.

All 125 shrine buildings will be taken down and rebuilt in the same form, and more than 1,500 sacred treasures — the deities' garments, ritual implements, and more — will be made anew. According to the Associated Press, the work of demolition and construction costs about $390 million. Through 33 festivals and ceremonies, the cycle builds toward its climax in 2033, when the deities move into the new shrines in the rite of Sengyo. The first cycle is recorded in 690, during the reign of Empress Jito, Noboru Okada, professor emeritus at Kogakkan University and a specialist in Japanese history and archaeology, told the Associated Press.

The cypress groves are planted specially for the Shikinen Sengu, and raising them can take longer than a single human lifetime. The thatch reed for the shrine roofs takes about eight years to grow to more than two meters long. Once a tree is cut with a special axe, it is wrapped in white cloth and straw mats. At night, priests carrying lanterns head into the mountains for a secret purification rite for the Shin-no-mihashira, the sacred pillar that will be enshrined beneath the floor of the main shrine.

As for why the cycle is 20 years, Ise Jingu itself says there is no established theory. The Associated Press report introduces the view that the cycle exists to pass building techniques and ritual forms reliably to the next generation, and the idea that it matches the stages of a human life — birth to maturity, maturity to middle age. About seven million people visit Ise Jingu each year, and many gather to witness the rites of the Shikinen Sengu.

For exam study, the key point is how the Shikinen Sengu works and how the 63rd cycle unfolds. The Shikinen Sengu is a rite held once every 20 years in which the shrine buildings and sacred treasures are all renewed so that Amaterasu Omikami may move into a new shrine. The 63rd cycle began in 2025 and continues through 33 festivals and ceremonies until the Sengyo transfer in 2033. The work is a single undertaking that includes not only rebuilding the shrines but also growing the timber, making the implements, and passing on the techniques.
