Nikko Toshogu Guide: The Three Wise Monkeys, the Sleeping Cat and the Carvings of Yomeimon Gate

Nikko Toshogu in Nikko City, Tochigi Prefecture, is a shrine enshrining Tokugawa Ieyasu as 'Tosho Daigongen.' Founded in the early Edo period, it is said to have been rebuilt into the present magnificent complex in the era of the third shogun, Tokugawa Iemitsu. In 1999 it was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List as part of the 'Shrines and Temples of Nikko.'

The main hall is gongen-zukuri — Toshogu's shrine buildings are the representative example of 'gongen-zukuri,' in which the main hall (honden) and the worship hall (haiden) are joined by a stone-floored chamber, the ishi-no-ma. As also covered in our article on architectural styles, it displays the decorative beauty of the early Edo period — lacquer, brilliant colors and carving — in contrast to the simplicity of older styles. Shrines enshrining Ieyasu as a deity, 'Toshogu,' exist in places such as Kunozan and Ueno as well, but Nikko is their center.

The Three Wise Monkeys are at the sacred stable — The carving of the Three Wise Monkeys, known for 'see no evil, speak no evil, hear no evil,' is on the lintel of the Shinkyusha, the stable for the sacred horses. It is one of eight carved panels depicting the life of a monkey, explained as the scene representing childhood. Monkeys were carved on a stable because of the custom of placing monkeys as protectors of horses.

The Sleeping Cat and the Imagined Elephant — Above the Sakashitamon Gate is the 'Sleeping Cat,' a small carving attributed to the master craftsman Hidari Jingoro. On its reverse side is a carving of sparrows, sometimes interpreted as a symbol of peace: because the cat sleeps, the sparrows can play without fear. The 'Imagined Elephant' at the Kaguraden hall is said to have been carved from imagination by a craftsman who had never seen an elephant, with the unnatural direction of its feet and the like told as evidence.

The carvings of Yomeimon Gate — Yomeimon, a National Treasure, is decorated with more than 500 carvings and is also known as the 'Gate of the Setting Sun' (Higurashi-no-mon, the gate you could gaze at until the sun goes down). Looking up from below, you will find one pillar whose pattern is upside down — the 'inverted pillar,' explained by the idea that a building should be left in an unfinished state to ward off calamity.

Exam study points — The focus is gongen-zukuri in Chapter 2 of the Shrine Exam textbook. Three standard pieces of knowledge: 'the deity of Nikko Toshogu is Tokugawa Ieyasu (Tosho Daigongen),' 'the Three Wise Monkeys are at the sacred stable,' and 'the Sleeping Cat is attributed to Hidari Jingoro.' Together with our article on Edo Castle, organize the relationship between the Tokugawa family and shrines.

Toshogu is a shrine where what you can see changes with the distance from which you look. As you walk the grounds, stop now and then and let your eyes linger on the details of the carvings.
