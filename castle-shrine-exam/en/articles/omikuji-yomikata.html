How to Read an Omikuji Fortune Slip: Why the Poem and the Details Matter More Than Your Luck Ranking

One of the pleasures of a shrine visit is the omikuji fortune slip. We tend to rejoice or despair the moment we draw one, over whether it is great luck or bad luck, but the true point of reading an omikuji is not just that single word of fortune.

【How Omikuji Began】Omikuji are said to have arisen from the tradition of drawing lots to ask the will of the gods. The form common today, with a poem and a fortune written on it, is sometimes traced to the 'Ganzan Daishi mikuji' associated with Ganzan Daishi (Ryogen): the mechanism of allotting a poem by the number of the lot you drew follows this line of tradition.

【The Order of Fortunes Differs by Shrine】The order following daikichi (great blessing) actually differs from shrine to shrine. The arrangement of chukichi, shokichi, kichi and suekichi is not uniform, nor are the kinds of 'kichi' or how 'kyo' (bad luck) is included. So rather than comparing the ranking itself with other shrines, the meaning lies in reading what is written on your own slip. Even if you draw a bad fortune, the traditional way to face an omikuji is to receive it as words urging care from here on.

【Read the Poem and the Items】Along with the general fortune, an omikuji carries a poem (a sacred song) and guidance item by item — wishes, a person you are waiting for, lost things, setting out on a journey, business, and so on. The correct way to read one is not to stop at the overall luck but to read through to the items that concern you. The poems are full of metaphor, so time spent slowly unraveling one within the precincts is part of the pleasure of omikuji.

【Tie It or Take It Home?】There is a custom of tying the omikuji you drew to a tying place in the precincts or to a tree branch before going home. Some explain tying as a way to leave a bad result behind in the grounds; others take theirs home whatever the result, to keep it as a reminder. Neither is the single correct rule — follow the guidance of the shrine or temple and you will be fine. You may also hear the saying that tying with the hand opposite your dominant one makes a wish harder to fulfill, and thus gives it greater meaning when it does come true.

【Exam Study Points】Omikuji belong to the culture of divination that asks the will of the gods, around Chapter 4, 'Festivals and Rituals,' of the Shrine Certification textbook. Organize them together with the etiquette of worship (Chapter 3) as part of the series of acts performed in the precincts, and your knowledge gains depth.

Next time you draw an omikuji, don't close it at the words daikichi or kyo alone — read through the poem and the item columns. In those short lines are hints for how to spend the season ahead.
