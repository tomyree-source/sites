Shichi-Go-San and Life's Milestones: From a Baby's First Shrine Visit to Yakudoshi

In November, shrine grounds fill with children in their best clothes, carrying bags of chitose-ame candy. Shichi-Go-San is a rite of shrine visiting deeply rooted in Japanese life. Centered on Shichi-Go-San, this article lays out the whole picture of 'life-cycle rites' — the shrine visits made at life's turning points.

The origins of Shichi-Go-San and its three celebrations — Shichi-Go-San celebrates milestones in a child's growth, giving thanks and reporting them to the deity. It is said to have been formed by combining older rites of childhood: 'kamioki' at age three, when children began to let their hair grow; 'hakama-gi' for boys of five, when a hakama was worn for the first time; and 'obi-toki' for girls of seven, when they began to tie an obi sash. The celebration season is generally said to be around November 15.

The meaning of chitose-ame and best clothes — Chitose-ame, the candy known as Shichi-Go-San's offering, is a long, thin candy imbued with a wish for long life, received in bags decorated with auspicious designs such as cranes and turtles. Dressing in one's finest and visiting the shrine is itself at the center of the rite: it shows the child's growth to the deity and to the family.

The first shrine visit of a life: omiyamairi — An earlier life-cycle rite than Shichi-Go-San is the 'omiyamairi' (hatsu-miyamairi), a newborn's first visit to the tutelary shrine. At around one month after birth, the family reports the birth and prays for healthy growth. Some regions also mark a milestone at about age thirteen with the 'jusan-mairi.' Yakubarai (the warding off of misfortune) is another representative milestone visit, made in step with the turning points of age; there are various theories about yakudoshi, the unlucky years, but they are widely explained in terms of milestones by traditional age reckoning.

Milestones in adult life — Coming-of-age visits and shrine weddings are also life-cycle rites held at shrines. A shrine wedding is a ceremony of vows before the enshrined deity, composed of traditional elements such as the shrine maiden's dance and the sansankudo cup exchange. Some people also visit shrines in step with celebrations of longevity in later life, such as kanreki and koki.

Exam study points — Related to Chapter 4 of the Shrine Exam textbook, 'Festivals and Rituals,' this is the face of shrines within everyday life. Organize the contrast — 'Shichi-Go-San celebrates growth at ages three, five and seven' versus 'omiyamairi is the visit reporting a birth' — and keep the viewpoint that life-cycle rites are reports made to the local tutelary deity.

For the child, a Shichi-Go-San visit is also a memory preserved in photographs. The custom of visiting the local shrine at family milestones is what has kept shrines close to everyday life.
