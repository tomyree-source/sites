How to Enjoy a Tour of Famous Shrines: Ise, Izumo and Ichinomiya Pilgrimage Routes

So far on this blog we have covered famous shrines across Japan: Ise Jingu, Izumo Taisha, Fushimi Inari Taisha, Itsukushima Shrine, Kasuga Taisha, Nikko Toshogu and Dazaifu Tenmangu. As a conclusion, this article gathers ways to enjoy visiting those shrines as a 'journey.'

Decide a theme and the trip takes shape — A shrine tour is easier to assemble if you decide on one theme. For 'a journey comparing architectural styles,' Ise in shinmei-zukuri, Izumo in taisha-zukuri and Nikko in gongen-zukuri; for 'a journey following deities,' Dazaifu and Kitano for Tenjin — and so on. The classifications you learned from the textbook become the blueprint for the trip itself.

The ichinomiya pilgrimage, a long-lasting journey — Touring the 'ichinomiya,' the shrines regarded as highest in standing in each old province, also enjoys enduring popularity. Visiting the central shrine of a region means touching the history of that land itself. As with castle touring for the 100 Famous Castles stamp rally, the knack is to continue little by little, without overdoing it. Overlay the two rallies, and the density of your travels rises sharply.

Castles and shrines are close at travel destinations — Castle towns often have a large shrine nearby, and combinations that let you tour a castle and a shrine in one day exist all over Japan: Izumo Taisha with Matsue Castle, Kasuga Taisha with a walk through Nara's streets, Dazaifu Tenmangu with the castle ruins of Fukuoka. The knowledge from this site's two exams can be used just as it is, for both, at your destination. Time spent tasting local specialties at shops along the approach is also an important part of the journey.

Things to check before you travel — Confirm festival days and event schedules in the shrine's official information before setting out. On the day of a reisai, the grounds bustle, but during the rites the areas open to the public may be limited. As a rule, follow each shrine's guidance on goshuin (shrine stamp) reception hours and on whether photography is permitted. Rereading our articles on shrine etiquette and on the summary of visiting manners before you leave will set your mind at rest.

Exam study points — The felt experience of travel is the best way to fix exam knowledge in memory. The style of a shrine building you saw in person, the history board you read, the impression of a festival you joined — these come back vividly even in the wording of exam questions. Study, travel, study again: that cycle is the way to improve at the Shrine Exam.

Start with one famous shrine nearby, and set out on a small journey with a theme decided. Exam study stops being something that exists only on your desk.
