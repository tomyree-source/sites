Shrine Rankings Explained: From Ichinomiya and Government Shrines to Beppyo Shrines

Among Japan's shrines, said to number about 80,000, some express their standing with words such as 'ichinomiya' or 'sohongu.' This article traces how shakaku — the idea of a shrine's rank and status — has changed from age to age.

Ancient rankings and the ichinomiya — In the age of the ritsuryo state, the court registered the shrines of the whole country in its registers of deities and maintained a system of presenting heihaku (sacred offerings) to important shrines. In time, the shrine regarded as highest in standing in each province (kuni, the old provincial units) came to be called the 'ichinomiya,' followed in some regions by ninomiya and sannomiya. Shrines calling themselves ichinomiya are still seen across Japan, indicating the center of faith in their land.

The modern system of government-supported shrines — In the Meiji era, shrines were placed under state administration and set in order. Kanpeisha, which received offerings from the Imperial Household, and kokuheisha, which received them from the state, were established, each with grades of taisha, chusha and shosha (large, medium and small). Further grades such as prefectural shrines, district shrines and village shrines were also created, extending official grading to local shrines as well. This modern ranking system lasted until the end of the Second World War.

The postwar beppyo shrine system — After the war, shrines left state administration, and most became religious corporations affiliated with Jinja Honcho (the Association of Shinto Shrines). Under today's Jinja Honcho, shrines of particular importance in history and scale are classed as 'beppyo shrines,' positioned within a framework that reveres Ise Jingu as its head shrine. The meaning of the word shakaku has changed with the times; today it is understood less as a hierarchy than as an indication of each shrine's lineage.

Names like sohongu and sohonsha — The centers of shrines worshipping the same deity — Fushimi Inari Taisha for Inari shrines, Dazaifu Tenmangu and Kitano Tenmangu for Tenjin shrines — are called 'sohongu' or 'sohonsha.' This is less an institutional rank than a name indicating the center of a faith's spread. When you visit and read the shrine's history board, you will always find these clues to its standing recorded there.

Exam study points — Surrounding knowledge for Chapters 1 and 5 of the Shrine Exam textbook. Take as basics the two points — 'the ichinomiya was the shrine regarded as highest in standing in each old province' and 'in the modern era there was a system of kanpeisha and kokuheisha' — and organize the flow up to the present system of beppyo shrines under Jinja Honcho.

A shrine's standing is not something for competing over height of rank; it is a marker of the depth of that land's and that shrine's history. Pay attention to a single line in the history board, and a visit gains in flavor.
