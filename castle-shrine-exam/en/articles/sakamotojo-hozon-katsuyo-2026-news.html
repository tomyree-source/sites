Sakamoto Castle Ruins in Otsu: Work Begins on a Preservation and Utilization Plan for Akechi Mitsuhide's “Phantom Castle”

This is a news article bringing you the latest developments concerning castles and shrines. Source: the Kyoto Shimbun (November 4, 2026, carried on Yahoo! News). From this autumn, Otsu City in Shiga Prefecture has begun to move toward the preservation and utilization of the Sakamoto Castle site (a National Historic Site), a castle said to have been built by the Sengoku warlord Akechi Mitsuhide. The city has set up a conference of experts well versed in history and landscape creation, and plans to formulate a preservation and utilization plan within fiscal 2027.

At the end of September, the first meeting of the “Conference for Studying the Drafting of the City Historic Site Sakamoto Castle Site Preservation and Utilization Plan” was held at a community center in Shimosakamoto, Otsu City. The five members include Professor Emerita Yuriko Yoshida of Tokyo University of Foreign Studies (history), Professor Shuichi Murakami of the University of Shiga Prefecture (landscape), and representatives of local neighborhood associations, with the Shiga Prefectural Cultural Properties Protection Section and the Agency for Cultural Affairs participating as observers. At the first meeting, the members inspected the Sakamoto Castle site on foot, and only the opening of the meeting was open to the public. The conference takes as its agenda the draft preservation and utilization plan presented by Otsu City; it will be held six times in total through next fiscal year, and the city aims to formulate the plan within next fiscal year after receiving the experts' opinions. Hidekazu Kawashima, head of Otsu City's Citizens' Department, greeted the first meeting by saying, “I sense potential in Sakamoto Castle in many different ways. We want to make active use of it in town development.”

The Sakamoto Castle site was designated a National Historic Site in September last year. It has been called a “phantom castle” because so few records and remains survive, but excavations found stone walls of the Sannomaru in fiscal 2023, and of the Honmaru this fiscal year. Professor Emeritus Hitoshi Nakai of the University of Shiga Prefecture (castle history), who serves as chair of the conference, spoke with enthusiasm: “It is called a phantom castle, but recent surveys have shown that remains survive underground. Because the surrounding area is residential, we want to make it a space where people can see the remains in some form, while being considerate of local residents' daily lives.”

This connects with themes in castle exam study. First, “National Historic Sites” — historic sites designated by the national government, in recognition of the castle site's historical value. Second, “Sakamoto Castle and Akechi Mitsuhide” — a castle said to have been built by Mitsuhide. The fewer records a castle has, the more dramatically excavation results can update its known form. Third, “preservation and utilization plans and historic site development” — the approach of not only protecting remains, but also arranging them as spaces people can view, with consideration for local daily life. The drafting of the plan itself is one of the procedures for passing a castle down to future generations.

A castle once called a “phantom” for its scarcity of records is gradually revealing itself through the discovery of stone walls and the drafting of a plan. When you visit the Sakamoto Castle site, check the visiting arrangements in Otsu City's announcements before you go.

A word from Tsurugi: Stone walls that had remained underground are found, and the planning begins to move — watching a “phantom castle” become a “visible castle” is itself one of the joys of castle touring. I want to keep following the remaining meetings of the six, too.
