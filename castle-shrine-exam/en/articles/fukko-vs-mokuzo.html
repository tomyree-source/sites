Reconstructed Keeps vs. Wooden Restoration Keeps: Concrete Castles and Castles Reborn from Historical Records

In the article on Osaka Castle we used the term 'fukko tenshu' (reconstructed keep). In fact, there are several kinds of rebuilt keep, and knowing the differences makes castle visits much more accurate.

【Reconstruction Keeps and Restoration Keeps】After the war, keeps were rebuilt in many places in reinforced concrete and the like. Those that reproduced the appearance before loss, based on clues to the exterior, are often called 'fukko tenshu' (reconstruction keeps) — the keep of Osaka Castle (1931) and the keep of Nagoya Castle (rebuilt 1959) are well known. On the other hand, those that reproduced the exterior based on historical materials such as excavation surveys, old photographs and drawings are called 'fukugen tenshu' (restoration keeps). Both are distinguished from 'genson tenshu,' keeps that survive from the Edo period.

【The Choice of Wooden Restoration】Increasing in recent years is 'mokuzo fukugen' — rebuilding keeps and turrets in traditional wood. These efforts aim to reproduce even the structure and interior based on historical materials; at Nagoya Castle, the Honmaru Palace was restored in wood, and wooden restoration of the keep has also been studied and planned. At Kumamoto Castle, too, wooden repair and restoration of turrets and other buildings has been carried out in the course of recovery.

【Why Rebuild at All?】Rebuilt keeps have been loved as symbols of their regions. At the same time, voices demanding accuracy as cultural properties have grown louder. There are examples of keeps restored in wood, such as Ozu Castle and Shiroishi Castle, and the way of rebuilding itself can be said to reflect the era's thinking about castles.

【Exam Study Points】'Fukugen tenshu' and 'mokuzo fukugen' in Chapter 5 of the textbook are the very points. Learn to distinguish four types — surviving keeps, reconstruction keeps, restoration keeps and wooden restoration keeps — together with representative examples.

When you visit a rebuilt keep, check on the information boards 'in what era, and with what thinking, it was rebuilt.' The keep itself is another historical document.
