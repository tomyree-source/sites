Miharu Castle Ruins in Fukushima: Earth Fill and Stone Walls Discovered in the Sannomaru Bailey Thought to Hold No Traces

This is a news article bringing you the latest developments concerning castles and shrines. Source: Fukushima Minyu Shimbun (October 18, 2026). In excavations at the “Miharu Castle site” in Miharu Town, Fukushima Prefecture, a town-designated historic site, earth fill and stone walls believed to date from the early Edo period were found in the remains of the castle's Sannomaru (third bailey). The Miharu Town Board of Education, which conducted the survey, presented the results at an on-site briefing held on October 17.

After the Meiji Restoration, the Sannomaru site was sold off to private hands and used as fields and the like, so it was thought that no traces of earlier periods remained. However, this survey found a layer of earth fill about 2 meters thick, believed to have been carried out in the early Edo period, on the south side of the Sannomaru site. The filling may have expanded the castle's grounds by more than 15 meters, and the town Board of Education describes it as a valuable trace showing how the castle was remodeled and repaired. A survey of a limited area of the Sannomaru site was carried out in 2001, but this is the first full-scale investigation.

The stone walls were built of roughly dressed stones, and at one corner, stone walls of neatly shaped stones were also found. The town Board of Education infers that the stone walls were repaired over the ages. An earthen rampart believed to have been built in the Sengoku period was also newly found. That traces built in different periods overlap in a single place speaks to the length of the castle's history.

This connects with themes in castle exam study. First, the “Sannomaru”: the bailey next in rank after the Honmaru and Ninomaru, offering a clue for grasping how castle compounds were organized. Second, “expanding the grounds by earth fill.” Piling up earth to widen flat ground is major construction that changes a castle's nawabari (layout). Third, “differences in stone wall construction by period.” From the difference between roughly dressed stones and neatly shaped stones, you develop the viewpoint of reading when walls were built and how they were repaired. Miharu Castle is a mountain castle said to have been built by the Sengoku warlord Tamura Yoshiaki, and is also called “Maizuru Castle” (Dancing Crane Castle), because cranes danced in the sky above the castle on the morning Yoshiaki entered it. The town Board of Education began excavations last fiscal year with support from the Agency for Cultural Affairs, and plans to continue surveys next fiscal year as well, aiming for designation as a National Historic Site.

From a place that had become fields, traces of the castle's remodeling come to light — each time a survey advances, the castle's known form is updated. When you visit the Miharu Castle site, check the visiting arrangements in Miharu Town's announcements before you go.
