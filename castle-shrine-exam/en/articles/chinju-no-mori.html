Chinju no Mori: Sacred Shrine Forests, Divine Trees and the Nature of the Precincts

In the middle of a residential area, there are places where a dense, deep green remains. In most cases, a shrine stands at their center. This article is about the shrine forest — the chinju no mori — and the nature preserved within shrine grounds.

What the chinju no mori is — The forest spreading around a shrine, as if to protect it, is called the 'chinju no mori' (tutelary shrine forest). Because felling was avoided out of reverence for the place where the deity resides, even in highly developed urban areas these forests remain precious spaces preserving the land's original vegetation. In botany, the forests of shrines and temples are called 'shaso' (shrine groves), and are studied as clues to the region's natural environment.

Sacred trees and shimenawa ropes — A particularly large old tree in the grounds is treated as a 'goshinboku,' a sacred tree, and protected with a shimenawa rope wound around it as a mark of the sacred. Camphor trees and ginkgos said to be hundreds of years old often tell the shrine's history itself. In the size and form of such trees, people have seen the form in which the deity dwells.

Faith that worships nature itself — Some shrines take not a building but a mountain, a waterfall or a rock itself as their object of worship, the goshintai. Nara's Omiwa Shrine is known for having Mount Miwa itself as its goshintai and possessing no main hall, preserving the old form of faith in which the mountain is worshipped from the worship hall. It shows that, before shrine buildings were raised, awe toward nature was the origin of belief.

Value as urban nature — The chinju no mori forms a habitat for birds, insects and other creatures, and it is also pointed out that it moderates the temperature of the area. That faith has, in effect, worked as a system of nature conservation is one reason these forests draw attention today. When you visit, turn your attention to the plants at your feet and the birdsong you can hear, and the grounds will look different.

Exam study points — A theme connected with the nature worship and ancient faith of Chapter 1 of the Shrine Exam textbook. 'Omiwa Shrine has Mount Miwa as its goshintai and no main hall' is asked on the exam. Keep the chinju no mori in mind as a concrete doorway into the Shinto view of nature.

Next time you visit a shrine, do not look only at the main hall — take a circuit of the trees in the grounds as well. You will begin to see what kind of nature that shrine has lived alongside.
