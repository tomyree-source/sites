Kuroi Castle Ruins in Tamba: Stone Walls Found at Four New Locations on the North Side Too — Possibly a Fully Stone-Walled Castle

This is a news article bringing you the latest developments concerning castles and shrines. Sources: Kobe Shimbun NEXT (November 2, 2026) and the Tamba Shimbun (dated October 31, 2026, carried on Yahoo! News). It has been learned, through a survey by the Social Education and Cultural Properties Section of the Tamba City Board of Education, that four new sections of stone wall have been found at the nationally designated historic site of the Kuroi Castle ruins in Tamba City, Hyogo Prefecture (Kuroi, Kasuga-cho, Tamba City).

The newly found walls are at four locations in total — the Honmaru, the Ninomaru, and two nearby kuruwa (baileys) — all on the north side, toward Ichijima-cho. All are “nozura-zumi,” built by stacking natural stones, and in some places the “uragome-ishi” (backing stones), packed behind the wall to raise drainage, are exposed. The walls had not been exposed because they were covered with grass and brushwood, and they were found during a survey measuring the existing stone walls and checking their damage. The stone walls can be viewed, but the Tamba City Board of Education asks visitors to “please not touch or climb them, as they may collapse.”

Until now, the stone walls were thought to have been built mainly on the south side (the Kasuga-cho side), where the castle town was. With stone walls now confirmed on the opposite, north side as well, the possibility has grown that this was a “so-ishigaki” castle, whose entire main compound — the “shukakubu” — was fortified with stone walls, and the finds are likely to become a clue for revising the castle's known form. Kuroi Castle is known as the castle of Ogino (Akai) Naomasa, the “Red Demon of Tamba” of the Sengoku period. It is said that there were no stone walls in the era when Naomasa was lord of the castle, and that they were built when the castle was remodeled after Akechi Mitsuhide's conquest of Tamba Province, in the eras of Mitsuhide's retainer Saito Toshizo and, later, of Horio Yoshiharu, a retainer of Hashiba Hideyoshi who entered the castle after him.

This connects with themes in castle exam study. First, “nozura-zumi”: an early-stage method of stacking stone walls, piling up natural stones with almost no dressing. Second, “uragome-ishi” (backing stones): stones packed behind a stone wall, which serve to raise drainage and stabilize the wall. Third, “so-ishigaki, the shukakubu and kuruwa.” The shukakubu is the compound forming the castle's core, and a kuruwa is a flat section divided off by earthen ramparts, stone walls and the like; if the entire shukakubu was fortified with stone walls, it becomes a major clue for thinking about how the castle expressed its defense and its authority. The Tamba City Board of Education intends, going forward, to analyze the measurement results of the stone walls, have evaluations and future development discussed at the Kuroi Castle Site Development Committee, to which experts belong, consult with related organizations, and then carry out excavations.

Stone walls that could not be seen, covered in vegetation, come to light in the course of measurement and damage surveys — a castle's known form is updated each time a survey advances. When you visit, please do not touch or climb the stone walls, and check the visiting arrangements in Tamba City's announcements before you go.

A word from Tsurugi: Stone walls we thought were only on the south side were on the north side too — the figure of the castle we took for its “face” becomes much more three-dimensional through surveys. Whether it was a fully stone-walled castle — I'm looking forward to the continuing investigation.
