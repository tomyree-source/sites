Local Sake (Jizake) Certification Study Guide

Learning About Local Sake and Brewery Culture

Chapter 1: The Basics of Sake

Sake Born from Rice, Water, and Koji

Japanese sake (seishu) is made by fermenting rice, rice koji, and water, and then filtering the result. Under the Liquor Tax Act, sake produced by such methods with an alcohol content of less than 22% is defined as "seishu." Most of the raw ingredients are rice and water, and the character of the local rice and water carries directly into the character of the local sake (jizake).

Rice suited to sake brewing is called "shuzo kotekimai" (sake-brewing rice), and representative varieties include Yamada Nishiki (Hyogo Prefecture and elsewhere), Gohyakumangoku (Niigata Prefecture and elsewhere), Miyama Nishiki (Nagano Prefecture and elsewhere), and Omachi (Okayama Prefecture and elsewhere). Rice with large grains and a "shinpaku" — a white core with little protein at the center — is considered to be of high quality.

The brewing water is also critically important. "Miyamizu" from Nada in Hyogo Prefecture has long been known as water suited to sake brewing; hard water containing minerals tends to produce powerful sake, while soft water tends to produce gentler sake. The nature of the water has shaped the tendencies of sake quality in each region.

Key points

  • The ingredients of seishu are rice, rice koji, and water, and its alcohol content is less than 22% (Liquor Tax Act)
  • Representative sake-brewing rice varieties are Yamada Nishiki, Gohyakumangoku, Miyama Nishiki, and Omachi
  • Nada's brewing water, "Miyamizu," is known as famous water for sake brewing

Chapter 2: The Sake Brewing Process

From Rice Polishing to Pressing

Sake brewing begins with "seimai" (rice polishing), which mills away the outer part of the brown rice. The degree of polishing is expressed by the "seimai-buai" (rice polishing ratio), and removing the proteins and lipids found in the outer part of the rice produces sake with fewer off-flavors. Next come washing and soaking the rice, and then steaming it; part of the steamed rice is used to cultivate koji mold to make "kome koji" (rice koji).

The "shubo" (yeast starter, also called moto) is prepared by adding yeast to rice koji, steamed rice, and water and cultivating it. The "moromi" (fermenting mash) in a large tank is grown through "sandan-jikomi" (three-stage preparation), in which steamed rice, koji, and water are added to the shubo in three separate additions and allowed to ferment. The process of pressing the moromi is called "joso" (pressing), and freshly pressed sake is normally stored and shipped after heat treatment (hi-ire, pasteurization).

The person with overall responsibility at the sake brewing site is called the "toji" (master brewer). Traditionally, sake brewing was based on "kan-zukuri" (winter brewing), carried out in the cold winter season, and groups of toji who traveled out to various regions during the agricultural off-season carried the techniques and flavors of each region.

Key points

  • The seimai-buai (rice polishing ratio) indicates the proportion of the rice that remains after polishing
  • Moromi is grown through sandan-jikomi (three-stage preparation)
  • The person with overall responsibility for sake brewing is the toji (master brewer)

Chapter 3: The Classification of Specially Designated Sake

Rice Polishing Ratio and Whether Brewer's Alcohol Is Added

Among Japanese sake, those that meet the requirements for ingredients and production methods are called "tokutei meisho-shu" (specially designated sake). The axes of classification are the rice polishing ratio and whether brewer's alcohol is added. Those without added brewer's alcohol carry the name "junmai" (pure rice).

A sake made only from rice and rice koji with a rice polishing ratio of 50% or less is "junmai daiginjo," and one at 60% or less is "junmai ginjo." Among those with a small amount of brewer's alcohol added, 50% or less is "daiginjo," 60% or less is "ginjo," 60% or less (or a special production method) is "tokubetsu honjozo" (special honjozo), and 70% or less is "honjozo." "Tokubetsu junmai" (special junmai) is a junmai sake at 60% or less, or one made by a special production method.

Note that "junmai sake" itself has no stipulated rice polishing ratio. The special designation written on the label is a blueprint of that sake's ingredients and brewing design, and it serves as a clue to the tendencies of its flavor.

Key points

  • Junmai types have no added brewer's alcohol
  • Daiginjo and junmai daiginjo have a rice polishing ratio of 50% or less
  • Honjozo has a rice polishing ratio of 70% or less

Chapter 4: Local Sake Producing Regions

Regional Character Nurtured by Water and Terroir

Nada in Hyogo Prefecture is one of Japan's representative sake-producing regions. Blessed with "Miyamizu," subterranean water from the Rokko mountain range, and the cold wind known as the "Rokko-oroshi," it was the center of "kudari-zake" — sake sent down to Edo — during the Edo period. Many breweries gather there, from major producers to long-established houses, including Kenbishi, Hakutsuru, Kikumasamune, Sawanotsuru, and Hakutaka.

Fushimi in Kyoto City is known for its townscape lined with sake breweries. Its high-quality subterranean water is called "Gokosui," and breweries such as Gekkeikan, Kizakura, Shoutoku, and Tamanohikari stand side by side. There is also a history of Nada sake and Fushimi sake being contrasted as "otoko-zake" (men's sake) and "onna-zake" (women's sake).

Niigata Prefecture is known for "tanrei karakuchi" (clean, light, and dry) sake, making use of the low-temperature environment unique to snow country and its strength as a rice-producing region; representative examples include Kubota from Asahi Shuzo, Hakkaisan from Hakkai Jozo, and Koshi no Kanbai from Ishimoto Shuzo. Saijo in Hiroshima Prefecture is a place associated with Miura Senzaburo, who spread the soft-water brewing method, and is known as a "sake capital" that contributed greatly to the development of ginjo brewing.

Key points

  • Nada's brewing water is Miyamizu, and Fushimi's subterranean water is Gokosui
  • Niigata sake is known for its tanrei karakuchi (clean, light, and dry) tendency
  • Saijo is a place of ginjo brewing associated with Miura Senzaburo

Chapter 5: How to Enjoy Sake and Read Labels

A World That Opens Up Through Temperature and Numbers

The flavor of Japanese sake changes with the temperature at which it is served. As rough guides, the temperature ranges have names such as reishu (chilled sake, around 5°C), hanabie (around 10°C), hinata-kan (around 30°C), hitohada-kan (around 35°C), nuru-kan (around 40°C), jo-kan (around 45°C), atsu-kan (around 50°C), and tobikiri-kan (around 55°C).

The "nihonshu-do" (sake meter value) on the label is an index based on specific gravity, and as a guide, values on the plus side tend to indicate a drier sake, while values on the minus side tend to indicate a sweeter one. "Acidity" (sando) and "amino acid content" (amino-sando) also provide clues to flavor tendencies. The way the aroma is perceived also changes depending on the vessel, such as a tokkuri and ochoko, or a wine glass.

Finally, an important promise: the legal drinking age in Japan is 20. Drinking by persons under 20 is prohibited by law (the Act on Prohibition of Drinking by Minors). Treat learning about local sake culture and drinking alcohol as separate things, and be sure to enjoy alcohol responsibly and in moderation.

Key points

  • Hitohada-kan is around 35°C and atsu-kan is around 50°C, as a guide
  • A sake meter value on the plus side is a guide to a drier tendency
  • The legal drinking age in Japan is 20 (drinking under the age of 20 is prohibited by law)

Practice questions

Twenty questions in the same style as the exam. Choose your answer first, then open the answer panel to check the explanation.

Q1. Which is the correct combination of ingredients for seishu? (Chapter 1: The Basics of Sake)

  1. Rice, rice koji, and water
  2. Barley, hops, and water
  3. Rice, malt, and water
  4. Sweet potato, rice koji, and water
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Rice, rice koji, and water

Seishu is made by fermenting rice, rice koji, and water, and then filtering it. Barley and hops are used for beer, and the ingredients differ from those of imo shochu (sweet potato shochu).

Q2. Under the Liquor Tax Act, what alcohol content range qualifies as "seishu"? (Chapter 1: The Basics of Sake)

  1. Less than 15%
  2. Less than 20%
  3. Less than 22%
  4. Less than 25%
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Less than 22%

Under the Liquor Tax Act, sake made by the prescribed methods with an alcohol content of less than 22% is defined as seishu.

Q3. Which is a representative variety of sake-brewing rice? (Chapter 1: The Basics of Sake)

  1. Koshihikari
  2. Yamada Nishiki
  3. Akitakomachi
  4. Hitomebore
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Yamada Nishiki

Yamada Nishiki is a representative variety of sake-brewing rice. Gohyakumangoku, Miyama Nishiki, and Omachi are also well known. Koshihikari and the others are mainly varieties grown for eating.

Q4. What is the famous water used for sake brewing in Nada? (Chapter 1: The Basics of Sake)

  1. Gokosui
  2. Miyamizu
  3. Oshino Water
  4. Ryusensui
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Miyamizu

Miyamizu is subterranean water from the Nishinomiya and Nada area of Hyogo Prefecture, and has long been known as water suited to sake brewing. Gokosui is the name of Fushimi's subterranean water.

Q5. What is steamed rice on which koji mold has been cultivated called? (Chapter 2: The Sake Brewing Process)

  1. Shubo (yeast starter)
  2. Rice koji (kome koji)
  3. Moromi
  4. Sake lees (sakekasu)
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Rice koji (kome koji)

Rice koji is made by cultivating koji mold on steamed rice, and it plays the role of converting the starch in the rice into sugar.

Q6. What is the common name of the preparation method used to grow moromi? (Chapter 2: The Sake Brewing Process)

  1. Two-stage preparation (nidan-jikomi)
  2. Three-stage preparation (sandan-jikomi)
  3. Four-stage preparation (yodan-jikomi)
  4. One-shot preparation
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Three-stage preparation (sandan-jikomi)

With sandan-jikomi (three-stage preparation), steamed rice, rice koji, and water are added to the shubo in three separate additions, growing the moromi little by little.

Q7. What is the person with overall responsibility at the sake brewing site called? (Chapter 2: The Sake Brewing Process)

  1. Kuramoto
  2. Toji
  3. Sakasho
  4. Koji-tsukasa
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Toji

The toji is the person who oversees the entire sake brewing process. Kuramoto refers to the owner who runs the brewery.

Q8. What is the rice polishing ratio requirement for junmai daiginjo? (Chapter 3: The Classification of Specially Designated Sake)

  1. 70% or less
  2. 60% or less
  3. 50% or less
  4. No stipulation
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. 50% or less

Junmai daiginjo and daiginjo require a rice polishing ratio of 50% or less. The ginjo types are 60% or less, and honjozo is 70% or less.

Q9. What condition is common to all sake with "junmai" in its name? (Chapter 3: The Classification of Specially Designated Sake)

  1. Its rice polishing ratio is 50% or less
  2. No brewer's alcohol is added
  3. It is unfiltered
  4. It is genshu (undiluted sake)
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. No brewer's alcohol is added

Junmai types of sake are made only from rice and rice koji, and the condition is that no brewer's alcohol is added.

Q10. What is the rice polishing ratio requirement for honjozo? (Chapter 3: The Classification of Specially Designated Sake)

  1. 50% or less
  2. 60% or less
  3. 70% or less
  4. 80% or less
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. 70% or less

Honjozo has a rice polishing ratio of 70% or less and is made with a small amount of brewer's alcohol added. Tokubetsu honjozo (special honjozo) is 60% or less.

Q11. What is the name of the subterranean water used for sake brewing in Fushimi? (Chapter 4: Local Sake Producing Regions)

  1. Miyamizu
  2. Gokosui
  3. Ten no Mizu
  4. Fushimi Water
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Gokosui

Fushimi's subterranean water is called "Gokosui" and is known as famous water. It stands alongside Nada's Miyamizu as the famous water of a sake-producing region.

Q12. Which phrase is known for describing the tendency of local sake from Niigata Prefecture? (Chapter 4: Local Sake Producing Regions)

  1. Tanrei karakuchi (clean, light, and dry)
  2. Nojun amakuchi (rich and sweet)
  3. Juko karakuchi (heavy and dry)
  4. Hojun amakuchi (mellow and sweet)
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Tanrei karakuchi (clean, light, and dry)

Niigata sake is known for its clean "tanrei karakuchi" (clean, light, and dry) tendency. It makes use of the low-temperature environment of snow country and its strength as a rice-producing region.

Q13. Who spread the soft-water brewing method in Saijo, Hiroshima Prefecture, and contributed to the development of ginjo brewing? (Chapter 4: Local Sake Producing Regions)

  1. Hideyo Noguchi
  2. Senzaburo Miura
  3. Eiichi Shibusawa
  4. Matsuo Basho
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Senzaburo Miura

Senzaburo Miura was a sake brewer of Saijo who devoted himself to spreading the soft-water brewing method, and he is also praised as the "father of ginjo sake."

Q14. In which prefecture is Takagi Shuzo, the brewery that makes "Juyondai"? (Chapter 4: Local Sake Producing Regions)

  1. Niigata Prefecture
  2. Yamagata Prefecture
  3. Akita Prefecture
  4. Fukushima Prefecture
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Yamagata Prefecture

Takagi Shuzo, the brewery of Juyondai, is located in Murayama City, Yamagata Prefecture. Yamagata Prefecture is also known for the fact that the entire prefecture has been designated under the geographical indication (GI) "Yamagata."

Q15. In which prefecture is Asahi Shuzo, the brewery that makes "Dassai"? (Chapter 4: Local Sake Producing Regions)

  1. Hiroshima Prefecture
  2. Yamaguchi Prefecture
  3. Shimane Prefecture
  4. Okayama Prefecture
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Yamaguchi Prefecture

Asahi Shuzo, the brewery of Dassai, is located in Iwakuni City, Yamaguchi Prefecture. It is known for sake brewing specialized in junmai daiginjo.

Q16. What is the guideline temperature for "hitohada-kan"? (Chapter 5: How to Enjoy Sake and Read Labels)

  1. About 30°C
  2. About 35°C
  3. About 45°C
  4. About 55°C
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. About 35°C

Hitohada-kan is about 35°C as a guide. Hinata-kan is about 30°C, nuru-kan is about 40°C, jo-kan is about 45°C, and atsu-kan is about 50°C.

Q17. What is the guideline temperature for "atsu-kan"? (Chapter 5: How to Enjoy Sake and Read Labels)

  1. About 35°C
  2. About 40°C
  3. About 50°C
  4. About 60°C
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. About 50°C

Atsu-kan is about 50°C as a guide. An even hotter temperature of about 55°C is called tobikiri-kan.

Q18. A sake with a sake meter value on the plus side is generally considered a guide to which tendency? (Chapter 5: How to Enjoy Sake and Read Labels)

  1. A sweeter tendency
  2. A drier tendency
  3. Strong acidity
  4. A mild aroma
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. A drier tendency

The sake meter value is an index based on specific gravity, and the plus side is taken as a guide to a drier tendency, while the minus side is a guide to a sweeter tendency.

Q19. What is the ball of cedar leaves hung at the eaves of a sake brewery to announce that the new sake is ready called? (Chapter 2: The Sake Brewing Process)

  1. Sugidama
  2. Matsubokkuri (pine cone)
  3. Hioogi (hinoki fan)
  4. Takekago (bamboo basket)
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Sugidama

A sugidama (also called sakabayashi) is made by bundling cedar leaves into a ball shape; it is green when first hung and changes to brown over time.

Q20. Which is correct regarding the age at which a person can drink alcohol in Japan? (Chapter 5: How to Enjoy Sake and Read Labels)

  1. From the age of 18
  2. From the age of 20
  3. After graduating from high school
  4. There is no restriction
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. From the age of 20

Drinking by persons under the age of 20 is prohibited by the Act on Prohibition of Drinking by Minors. The legal drinking age in Japan is 20 — enjoy alcohol only after you turn 20.
