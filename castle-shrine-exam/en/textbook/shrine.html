Chapter 1: The Basics of Shrines and Shinto

How the places that enshrine the kami came to be

Shinto is a faith nurtured in Japan's natural environment, enshrining nature — mountains, rivers, rocks, trees — and ancestral spirits as kami (deities). Because the kami are countless, they are described as the 'eight million kami' (yaoyorozu no kami).

A shrine is a facility that enshrines a kami, and there are said to be about 80,000 shrines across Japan. Many shrines are under the umbrella of the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honcho), but some, such as Ise Jingu, exist independently. Shrines recognized by the imperial court in ancient times as shikinaisha are still known today as shrines of high rank.

The object in which a kami resides is called a goshintai (sacred body). It may be a mirror, a sword, or the like, but at Omiwa Shrine in Nara Prefecture, Mount Miwa itself is the goshintai, and the shrine has no honden (main sanctuary). The guardian deity of a local area is called the ujigami, and the people living in that area are called ujiko.

Key points

  • There are about 80,000 shrines across Japan
  • A goshintai is not necessarily an object — it can be a mountain itself
  • The local guardian deity is the ujigami, and the residents are ujiko

Chapter 2: Shrine Architecture

Forms born from ancient granaries

Many honden (main sanctuary) architectural styles are considered to derive from the forms of ancient granaries and palaces. A representative style is shinmei-zukuri, typified by the shrine buildings of Ise Jingu. Its features are a plain yet powerful form: a gabled roof fitted with chigi (forked finials) and katsuogi (short horizontal logs along the ridge).

The taisha-zukuri of Izumo Taisha is one of the oldest styles, said to preserve the appearance of ancient palace architecture; its honden is raised on pillars, with the entrance on the gable side. Nagare-zukuri is the style seen most widely at shrines across Japan, with the front of the roof extending long to cover the kohai (worship space).

Hachiman-zukuri, common at Hachiman shrines, is a composite structure with a front hall and a rear hall set side by side, while gongen-zukuri, known from Toshogu shrines, is an ornate style that connects the honden and the haiden (worship hall) with a stone-floored room (ishi-no-ma). Roofing materials include thatch, cypress bark, and copper plate, varying by region and rank.

Key points

  • Shinmei-zukuri = Ise Jingu; taisha-zukuri = Izumo Taisha
  • The most widely seen style is nagare-zukuri
  • Chigi and katsuogi are the markers of a honden roof

Chapter 3: Walking the Shrine Grounds

From the torii gate to the main sanctuary

The torii gate standing at a shrine's entrance marks the boundary between the realm of the kami and the human world. The top crossbeam is called the kasagi, the beam below it the shimaki, and the crossbeam passing below that the nuki. Torii come in families of forms, including the shinmei type, whose kasagi is straight, and the myojin type, whose kasagi has an upward curve.

The path from the torii toward the honden is the sando (approach). The center of the sando is considered the seichu, the path the kami travel, so etiquette is to walk slightly toward the edge. Stone lanterns stand along the sando, and in front of the honden is a pair of komainu (guardian dog statues): the a-gyo with its mouth open and the un-gyo with its mouth closed.

Before praying, purify your hands and mouth at the temizuya (purification basin). The procedure: hold the ladle in your right hand and purify your left hand; switch the ladle to the other hand and purify your right hand; receive water in your left hand and rinse your mouth; finally, hold the ladle upright to purify its handle. In front of the honden, the general etiquette is to put in a coin offering, ring the bell, and pray with two bows, two claps, and one bow.

Key points

  • The top of a torii is the kasagi and shimaki; the crossbeam is the nuki
  • Komainu are a pair: a-gyo (mouth open) and un-gyo (mouth closed)
  • Prayer is two bows, two claps, one bow; purification starts with the left hand

Chapter 4: Festivals and Sacred Rites

Events that welcome and entertain the kami

Shrine festivals are held throughout the seasons, centered on the reisai (also called reitaisai), the most important festival of the year held once a year, together with rites matched to the rice-growing cycle such as the Kinen-sai and the Niiname-sai. The event in which a mikoshi (portable shrine) parades through the parishioners' town is called the shinko-sai (togyo procession).

Known as Japan's three great festivals are the Gion Matsuri in Kyoto, the Tenjin Matsuri in Osaka, and the Kanda Matsuri in Tokyo. All have histories of nearly a thousand years and are urban festivals thronged with people.

At Ise Jingu, the Shikinen Sengu — rebuilding the shrine buildings anew and transferring the kami once every 20 years — has been continued for nearly 1,300 years. It is Ise Jingu's greatest rite, and it also serves to pass techniques and traditions on to the next generation.

Key points

  • The most important festival is the reisai; the mikoshi procession is the shinko-sai
  • The three great festivals are the Gion Matsuri, Tenjin Matsuri, and Kanda Matsuri
  • Ise Jingu's Shikinen Sengu takes place once every 20 years

Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan

The leading shrines you should know

Ise Jingu in Mie Prefecture is the foremost of all shrines, centered on the Naiku, which enshrines Amaterasu Omikami, and the Geku, which enshrines Toyo'uke no Omikami, and is affectionately known as 'O-Ise-san.' Izumo Taisha in Shimane Prefecture enshrines Okuninushi no Okami, and because in the 10th month of the lunar calendar the kami from all over Japan are said to gather in Izumo, the Izumo region calls that month Kamiari-zuki ('the month when the kami are present').

Itsukushima Shrine in Hiroshima Prefecture is known for its shrine buildings standing over the sea and is registered as a World Cultural Heritage site. Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyoto is the head shrine of all Inari shrines nationwide, and its approach lined with many torii gates — the Senbon Torii — is a highlight.

Usa Jingu in Oita Prefecture is the head shrine of all Hachiman shrines nationwide, and Kasuga Taisha in Nara enshrines the ujigami of the Fujiwara clan; the deer in its grounds are cherished as shinroku, messengers of the kami. Kashima Jingu in Ibaraki and Katori Jingu in Chiba are ancient shrines long praised side by side as guardians of the eastern provinces.

Key points

  • Ise Jingu = Naiku and Geku, 'O-Ise-san'
  • Izumo has Kamiari-zuki; Itsukushima Shrine's buildings over the sea are a World Heritage site
  • The head shrine of Hachiman is Usa Jingu; of Inari, Fushimi Inari Taisha

Practice questions

Twenty questions in the same style as the exam. Choose your answer first, then open the answer panel to check the explanation.

Q1. How often is the Shikinen Sengu held at Ise Jingu? (Chapter 4: Festivals and Sacred Rites)

  1. Every 10 years
  2. Every 20 years
  3. Every 33 years
  4. Every 60 years
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Every 20 years

The Shikinen Sengu is the rite, held once every 20 years, of rebuilding the shrine buildings and transferring the goshintai to the new sanctuary; it has continued for nearly 1,300 years, since the end of the Nara period.

Q2. Which deity is enshrined in the Naiku (Inner Shrine) of Ise Jingu? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. Okuninushi no Okami
  2. Amaterasu Omikami
  3. Toyo'uke no Omikami
  4. Ukanomitama no Okami
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Amaterasu Omikami

The deity of the Naiku is Amaterasu Omikami, regarded as the ancestral deity of the Imperial Family. Toyo'uke no Omikami is the deity of the Geku.

Q3. Which deity is enshrined at Izumo Taisha? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. Okuninushi no Okami
  2. Sukunahikona no Kami
  3. Takeminakata no Kami
  4. Amenominakanushi no Kami
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Okuninushi no Okami

The deity of Izumo Taisha is Okuninushi no Okami, known as the deity of nation-building.

Q4. What is the topmost crossbeam of a torii gate called? (Chapter 3: Walking the Shrine Grounds)

  1. Nuki
  2. Shimaki
  3. Kasagi
  4. Gakuzuka
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Kasagi

The topmost crossbeam of a torii is the kasagi; the beam below it is the shimaki, and the crossbeam passing through the pillars is the nuki.

Q5. Of a pair of komainu, what is the one with its mouth open called? (Chapter 3: Walking the Shrine Grounds)

  1. Un-gyo
  2. A-gyo
  3. Nio-gyo
  4. Shishi-gyo
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. A-gyo

The pair consists of the open-mouthed 'a-gyo' and the closed-mouth 'un-gyo,' and 'a-un' is said to represent the beginning and the end of all things.

Q6. In the temizu purification rite, which hand do you purify first? (Chapter 3: Walking the Shrine Grounds)

  1. The right hand
  2. The left hand
  3. Both hands at the same time
  4. The mouth
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The left hand

Hold the ladle in your right hand and purify your left hand first, then switch the ladle to the other hand and purify your right hand — that is the proper procedure.

Q7. What is the general etiquette for praying at a shrine? (Chapter 3: Walking the Shrine Grounds)

  1. One bow, one clap, one bow
  2. Two bows, two claps, one bow
  3. Two bows, three claps, one bow
  4. Three bows, two claps, one bow
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Two bows, two claps, one bow

Two bows, two claps, and one bow is the general etiquette. Some shrines, such as Izumo Taisha, pray with four claps.

Q8. What are the white, lightning-shaped pieces of paper hanging from a shimenawa rope called? (Chapter 3: Walking the Shrine Grounds)

  1. Shide
  2. Hei
  3. Tamagushi
  4. Shinsatsu
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Shide

Shide are decorations marking a sacred place, and are attached to shimenawa ropes, tamagushi offerings, kagura bells, and the like.

Q9. Which is correct as the deity enshrined at Itsukushima Shrine? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. The Three Munakata Goddesses
  2. Konohanasakuya-hime
  3. Iwanaga-hime no Mikoto
  4. Ame-no-Uzume no Mikoto
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. The Three Munakata Goddesses

Itsukushima Shrine enshrines the Three Munakata Goddesses, including Ichikishima-hime no Mikoto, and has been worshipped as a deity of the sea.

Q10. What is the principal deity of Fushimi Inari Taisha? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. Hoshoku no Kami (Ukemochi no Kami)
  2. Ukanomitama no Okami
  3. Omiyanome no Okami
  4. Toyo'uke-hime no Kami
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Ukanomitama no Okami

At the center of the Inari deity is Ukanomitama no Okami, enshrined as the deity of rice, food, and abundance.

Q11. Which shrine is the representative example of shinmei-zukuri? (Chapter 2: Shrine Architecture)

  1. Izumo Taisha
  2. Ise Jingu
  3. Nikko Toshogu
  4. Itsukushima Shrine
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Ise Jingu

The honden of Ise Jingu is the representative example of shinmei-zukuri. Izumo Taisha is taisha-zukuri, and Nikko Toshogu is gongen-zukuri.

Q12. Which is the correct pair of ornaments fitted to a honden roof? (Chapter 2: Shrine Architecture)

  1. Chigi and katsuogi
  2. Shachihoko and onigawara
  3. Gegyo and koryo
  4. Koran and giboshi
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Chigi and katsuogi

Chigi are the members that cross at both ends of the roof, and katsuogi are the members lined along the ridge; they are hallmarks of shinmei-zukuri and taisha-zukuri.

Q13. Which is the head shrine of all Hachiman shrines nationwide? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. Tsurugaoka Hachimangu
  2. Iwashimizu Hachimangu
  3. Usa Jingu
  4. Hakozaki-gu
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Usa Jingu

Usa Jingu in Oita Prefecture is the head shrine of the Hachiman shrines, which are said to number about 40,000 nationwide.

Q14. At Kasuga Taisha, which animal is cherished as a messenger of the kami? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. Crane
  2. Deer
  3. Turtle
  4. Fox
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Deer

The deity of Kasuga Taisha is said to have arrived riding a white deer, and the deer of Nara have been protected since ancient times as 'sacred deer' (shinroku).

Q15. Which shrine takes Mount Miwa itself as its goshintai and has no honden? (Chapter 1: The Basics of Shrines and Shinto)

  1. Omiwa Shrine
  2. Isonokami Jingu
  3. Oyamato Shrine
  4. Kashihara Jingu
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Omiwa Shrine

Omiwa Shrine in Nara Prefecture preserves the ancient form of faith — worshipping Mount Miwa from its haiden — and has no honden.

Q16. What is the event called in which a mikoshi parades through the parishioners' district? (Chapter 4: Festivals and Sacred Rites)

  1. Kami-mukae-sai
  2. Shinko-sai
  3. Chinka-sai
  4. Otaue-sai
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Shinko-sai

The shinko-sai (togyo) is the event in which the divine spirit is transferred to a mikoshi and parades through the district — a splendid highlight of the festival.

Q17. Which is the correct combination of Japan's three great festivals? (Chapter 4: Festivals and Sacred Rites)

  1. Gion Matsuri, Tenjin Matsuri, and Kanda Matsuri
  2. Gion Matsuri, Aoi Matsuri, and Jidai Matsuri
  3. Tenjin Matsuri, Sanja Matsuri, and Sanno Matsuri
  4. Kanda Matsuri, Gion Matsuri, and Aomori Nebuta Matsuri
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Gion Matsuri, Tenjin Matsuri, and Kanda Matsuri

Kyoto's Gion Matsuri, Osaka's Tenjin Matsuri, and Tokyo's Kanda Matsuri are regarded as Japan's three great festivals.

Q18. Which is the title of the Shinto priest who is the chief of a shrine? (Chapter 1: The Basics of Shrines and Shinto)

  1. Jushoku
  2. Guji
  3. Shinshu-gashira
  4. Shake
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Guji

The guji is the Shinto priest with the highest responsibility at a single shrine. 'Jushoku,' by contrast, is a post at a Buddhist temple.

Q19. In the Izumo region, what is the 10th month of the lunar calendar — when the kami from all over Japan are said to gather — called? (Chapter 5: Famous Shrines Across Japan)

  1. Kannazuki
  2. Kamiari-zuki
  3. Kami-mukae-zuki
  4. Kami-gaeri-zuki
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Kamiari-zuki

The 10th month of the lunar calendar, called Kannazuki in other regions, is called Kamiari-zuki in Izumo because the kami gather there.

Q20. What is the most important annual festival of a shrine? (Chapter 4: Festivals and Sacred Rites)

  1. Saitan-sai
  2. Reisai
  3. Tsukinami-sai
  4. Niiname-sai
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Reisai

The reisai (retaisai), held on a day deeply connected with the shrine, is its most important festival.
