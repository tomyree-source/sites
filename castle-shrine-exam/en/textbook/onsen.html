Chapter 1: What Is an Onsen?

The conditions for an “onsen” under the Hot Springs Act

Onsen are defined by a law called the Hot Springs Act. Hot water, mineral water, steam, and other substances that emerge from underground are recognized as onsen if the temperature at the point of emergence is 25°C or higher, or if, even at a lower temperature, they contain at least a specified amount of one of the prescribed components, such as lithium ions or metasilicic acid.

Japan is a volcanic country, and according to statistics from the Ministry of the Environment, there are approximately 27,000 hot spring sources and approximately 3,000 hot spring areas. The system in which spring water, just as it emerges, is poured continuously into the bath without being circulated or filtered is called “gensen kakenagashi” (free-flowing from the source). On the other hand, where the volume of water is limited, for example, the “circulation type,” in which the water in the bath is circulated and filtered for use, is also widely used.

If the temperature of a source is too high, water may be added to dilute it (“adding water”), and if it is too low, the water may be heated (“heating”). Notices at hot spring areas and hot spring analysis certificates list such information as the source's spring quality and temperature, and whether it is free-flowing.

Key points

  • Under the Hot Springs Act, the condition is a water temperature of 25°C or higher at the point of emergence, or the presence of a prescribed component
  • Japan has approximately 27,000 hot spring sources and approximately 3,000 hot spring areas
  • Gensen kakenagashi is the system of pouring spring water directly into the bath without circulation or filtration

Chapter 2: The Classification of Spring Qualities

The character of the water is determined by its components

The spring quality of an onsen is classified according to the components it contains. Those that meet the component standards as therapeutic springs in particular are divided into 10 types: simple hot springs, chloride springs, hydrogen carbonate springs, sulfate springs, carbon dioxide springs, iron-containing springs, sulfur springs, acid springs, radioactive springs, and iodine-containing springs.

For example, a chloride spring is water containing salts such as table salt, and it is often found in hot spring areas near the sea. A sulfur spring is characterized by the smell of sulfur, and its water may turn milky white and cloudy. A radioactive spring is the name of a classification for water containing extremely small amounts of natural radium or radon.

The nature of the water is expressed by pH: below 3 is acidic, 3 or more but less than 6 is weakly acidic, 6 or more but less than 7.5 is neutral, 7.5 or more but less than 8.5 is weakly alkaline, and 8.5 or more is alkaline. Even within the same hot spring area, the spring quality may differ from source to source, so checking the displayed information is the first step in touring hot springs.

Key points

  • Therapeutic springs are classified into 10 types in total
  • Chloride springs are waters containing salt, and sulfur springs are characterized by the smell of sulfur
  • A pH of 7.5 or more but less than 8.5 is weakly alkaline

Chapter 3: The History of Onsen and Toji (the Hot Spring Cure)

A long-standing culture of soaking in hot water

The use of onsen in Japan is ancient, and legends from ancient times remain at places such as Arima Onsen and Dogo Onsen. “Toji,” staying in a hot spring area for an extended period to recuperate, took root in various places by the Edo period as a way of life in which farmers during the agricultural off-season and travelers would stay for several weeks.

The Edo-period Confucian scholar Hayashi Razan is said to have named Kusatsu Onsen (Gunma Prefecture), Gero Onsen (Gifu Prefecture), and Arima Onsen (Hyogo Prefecture) as outstanding hot springs, and these three places are called Japan's Three Great Hot Springs. In addition, Arima Onsen, Dogo Onsen (Ehime Prefecture), and Shirahama Onsen (Wakayama Prefecture), which are known for their great age, are called Japan's Three Ancient Hot Springs.

From the Meiji period onward, as railways developed, hot spring travel spread, and onsen also took on a stronger character as a pleasure of travel, rather than only a place for recuperation. Communal bathhouses and public outdoor baths (sotoyu) in hot spring areas are still cherished today as places that connect local people with visitors.

Key points

  • Japan's Three Great Hot Springs are Kusatsu, Gero, and Arima (named by Hayashi Razan)
  • Japan's Three Ancient Hot Springs are Arima, Dogo, and Shirahama
  • Toji is the custom of staying in a hot spring area for an extended period to recuperate

Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Hot Springs

The faces of distinctive hot spring areas

At the center of Kusatsu Onsen is the “Yubatake,” a facility in which the large volume of spring water that emerges every minute is run through wooden flumes to lower its temperature and to collect yunohana (hot spring mineral deposits); it is the symbol of the hot spring town. “Yumomi,” stirring the hot water in time with a song to cool it, is also known as a specialty of Kusatsu.

Beppu Onsen in Oita Prefecture is a hot spring area said to have the largest number of sources and the largest volume of discharge in Japan, and the group of hot springs known as the Beppu Hatto (Eight Hot Springs of Beppu) spreads across the city. The “Jigoku Meguri” (Hell Tour), visiting the vividly colored source ponds, is another specialty. At Noboribetsu Onsen in Hokkaido, waters of diverse spring qualities are supplied from “Jigokudani” (Hell Valley) in the source area.

Kinosaki Onsen in Hyogo Prefecture is known for “sotoyu meguri,” touring its seven communal bathhouses, the sotoyu (public outdoor baths), in a yukata. At Dogo Onsen in Ehime Prefecture, the Dogo Onsen Honkan, a nationally designated Important Cultural Property, is the face of the hot spring town. Local communal bathhouses and foot baths are an entrance to experiencing life in a hot spring area.

Key points

  • At the center of Kusatsu Onsen is the Yubatake, and yumomi is also a specialty
  • Beppu Onsen is said to have the largest number of sources and volume of discharge in Japan
  • Kinosaki Onsen is known for touring its seven sotoyu, and the Dogo Onsen Honkan is an Important Cultural Property

Chapter 5: Bathing Etiquette and Precautions

To soak safely and comfortably

Before bathing, the etiquette is to pour hot water over yourself (kakeyu) to wash away dirt from your body and to accustom your body to the temperature of the water. Washing your body before getting into the bath, and not putting your towel into the bath, are considered basic manners at a communal bathhouse.

Continuing to soak for a long time in hot water can cause lightheadedness and yuatari (feeling unwell after bathing). Bathe in reasonable periods of time, and replenish fluids before and after bathing. Bathing immediately after a meal, or bathing after drinking alcohol, places a heavy burden on the body, so it is safer to avoid them.

At hot springs where the temperature of the source is extremely high, methods such as adjusting the temperature with water, or cooling the water by yumomi, have been handed down. Checking the hot spring analysis certificate and the notices at the bathhouse is also an important part of enjoying onsen safely.

Key points

  • Before bathing, pour water over yourself (kakeyu), and do not put your towel in the bath
  • Avoid long baths, and replenish fluids before and after bathing
  • Avoid bathing after drinking alcohol

Q19. Which is a correct precaution when bathing? (Chapter 5: Bathing Etiquette and Precautions)

Correct answer: C. Replenish fluids before and after bathing
