Chapter 1: The Origins and Types of Castles

Castles take different forms depending on where they stand

Japanese castles developed greatly during the Sengoku (Warring States) period. The basic classification is: yamajiro ("mountain castles"), which use the mountain terrain itself for defense; hirayamajiro ("hill castles"), built on hills or plateaus; and hirajiro ("flatland castles"), built on level ground. There are also mizujiro ("water castles," also called sea castles), which face the sea or a lake and combine water transport with defense.

Early mountain castles were earthen fortresses built to keep enemies out. Builders raised earthen ramparts, dug dry moats, and cut into slopes to run vertical moats (tatebori) and horizontal moats (yokobori) across them. In time, castles with stone walls and tile-roofed buildings appeared, and with Azuchi Castle — whose construction Oda Nobunaga began in 1576 — the prototype of the early modern castle, complete with a main keep, is said to have been born.

In the Edo period, castles became not only military strongholds but also centers of domain government and symbols of authority. Their locations shifted from mountain castles to hill castles and flatland castles, and they came to be developed together with the castle towns around them.

Key points

  • The basic classification has four types: mountain castles, hill castles, flatland castles and water castles
  • Azuchi Castle was the starting point of the early modern castle
  • Castles changed from defensive facilities into government centers and symbols of authority

Chapter 2: Main Keeps and Turrets

The face of the castle — and its final stronghold

The main keep (tenshu) is the tall building towering at the center of a castle. It served as a lookout and command post, and at the same time was a symbol displaying the castle lord's authority. On the top of its roof sit shachihoko, ornaments imbued with wishes to ward off fire and evil.

Main keeps come in two broad forms: boro-gata ("lookout type") and soto-gata ("layered tower type"). The boro-gata is the earlier form, in which a lookout turret is placed on top of a building with an irimoya (hip-and-gable) roof; Himeji Castle and Inuyama Castle are representative examples. The soto-gata decreases in size in regular steps from the lower stories to the upper ones; Matsumoto Castle is a well-known example.

There are also different ways of combining a large keep with small keeps: renketsu-shiki ("connected type"), in which they are joined by a connecting turret (watari-yagura); renritsu-shiki ("linked type"), in which several keeps are joined directly to one another; and dokuritsu-shiki ("independent type"), in which the keep stands alone. Himeji Castle is the representative of the renritsu-shiki: its large keep and three small keeps are joined by connecting turrets.

Guarding the flanks of the main keep are the turrets (yagura). Two-story and three-story turrets were placed at key points along the castle walls, and inside they were used as armories and lookout posts.

Key points

  • Shachihoko are ornaments that ward off fire and evil
  • Keep forms are boro-gata and soto-gata; keeps are joined in the renketsu, renritsu or dokuritsu styles
  • Only Himeji Castle and Matsumoto Castle still have five-story main keeps

Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts

Reading the skeleton of a castle

Stone-wall building techniques evolved over time, growing more advanced in this order: nozura-zumi, which stacks natural stones almost as they are; uchikomi-hagi, which dresses the joint faces of the stones to reduce gaps; and kirikomi-hagi, which shapes the stones precisely and stacks them without gaps.

The corners of a stone wall are strengthened with sangi-zumi, a technique that alternates long rectangular stones. Stone walls whose gradient grows steeper toward the top and curves back are called musha-gaeshi ("warrior repellers"); Kumamoto Castle's "two-style stone walls" are famous.

The castle's equivalent of a blueprint is its nawabari (layout). The central honmaru (main bailey) is surrounded by enclosures (kuruwa) such as the ni-no-maru (second bailey) and san-no-maru (third bailey), and is defended by wet moats, dry moats and earthen ramparts. The entrances are called koguchi ("tiger mouths"); they were made to bend so that enemies could not charge straight through, and a square space called a masugata was set inside to halt intruders.

Key points

  • The evolution of stacking: nozura-zumi → uchikomi-hagi → kirikomi-hagi
  • Corners use sangi-zumi; walls that curve back at the top are musha-gaeshi
  • Koguchi entrances and masugata spaces slow down enemy intrusion

Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles

Surviving keeps and National Treasure castles

The "twelve surviving keeps" — keeps of castles built before the end of the Edo period that still stand today — are those of Hirosaki Castle, Matsumoto Castle, Maruoka Castle, Inuyama Castle, Hikone Castle, Himeji Castle, Matsue Castle, Bitchu-Matsuyama Castle, Marugame Castle, Matsuyama Castle, Uwajima Castle and Kochi Castle. Of these, the keeps of five castles — Himeji, Matsumoto, Inuyama, Hikone and Matsue — are designated National Treasures.

Himeji Castle, with its white-plastered appearance, bears the nickname Hakuro-jo ("White Heron Castle"), and in 1993 it became one of the first sites in Japan to be registered as a UNESCO World Cultural Heritage site. Matsumoto Castle, with its black keep, is also called Karasu-jo ("Crow Castle") and preserves its appearance from the Sengoku period.

Kumamoto Castle is a famous castle built by Kato Kiyomasa. It suffered great damage in the 2016 Kumamoto earthquakes, but restoration has progressed and the main keep's restoration was completed in 2021. Castles live on today as symbols of their regions.

Key points

  • Twelve surviving keeps; five are National Treasures (Himeji, Matsumoto, Inuyama, Hikone, Matsue)
  • Himeji Castle = White Heron Castle, a World Cultural Heritage site
  • Matsumoto Castle = Crow Castle; Kumamoto Castle was built by Kato Kiyomasa

Chapter 5: Castle Sieges and the End of Castles

How castles came to the end of their role

In 1615, the Edo shogunate issued the Ikkoku Ichijo Rei (One Castle per Province Order), which had all castles other than the daimyo's main residence demolished. In addition, the Buke Shohatto (Laws for the Military Houses) required shogunate permission for building new castles or repairing existing ones, and the number of castles fell sharply.

With the Meiji era came the Haijo Rei (Castle Abolition Order) of 1873, and many castles were torn down. Even castles that survived were used as army grounds or sold off, and they disappeared one after another.

Voices lamenting the loss of castles gave rise to preservation movements, and through laws such as the National Treasure Preservation Law, castles entered an era in which they are protected as cultural properties. For keeps that were lost, efforts are also under way across Japan: "restored keeps" (fukugen tenshu), which recreate the exterior based on historical materials, and "wooden reconstructions" (mokuzo fukugen), which rebuild even the interior in wood.

Key points

  • 1615: castles were consolidated under the One Castle per Province Order
  • 1873: many castles were dismantled under the Castle Abolition Order
  • Today is an era of preservation and restoration as cultural properties

Practice questions

Twenty questions in the same style as the exam. Choose your answer first, then open the answer panel to check the explanation.

Q1. Which of the following is a nickname of Himeji Castle? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. Crow Castle
  2. White Heron Castle
  3. Shachihoko Castle
  4. Tsuruga Castle
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. White Heron Castle

Himeji Castle is called the "White Heron Castle" because its figure, coated in white plaster, resembles a white heron. "Crow Castle" refers to Matsumoto Castle (and Okayama Castle as well).

Q2. Which ornament, placed on the roof of a main keep, is believed to ward off fire and evil? (Chapter 2: Main Keeps and Turrets)

  1. Shachihoko
  2. Onigawara
  3. Kengyo
  4. Shibi
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Shachihoko

The shachihoko is an imaginary creature believed to spout water and extinguish fires; it was placed at both ends of the keep's roof as a guardian of the main keep.

Q3. Which is the oldest stone-wall technique, in which natural stones are stacked almost as they are? (Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts)

  1. Kirikomi-hagi
  2. Uchikomi-hagi
  3. Nozura-zumi
  4. Sangi-zumi
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Nozura-zumi

Nozura-zumi is a technique that stacks natural stones with little dressing. As time passed, the techniques became more precise, moving to uchikomi-hagi and then kirikomi-hagi.

Q4. Which technique strengthens the corners of a stone wall by alternating long rectangular stones? (Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts)

  1. Musha-gaeshi
  2. Sangi-zumi
  3. Nunozumi
  4. Ranzumi
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Sangi-zumi

Sangi-zumi alternately combines long and short stones at the protruding corners of a stone wall, creating corners that withstand loads well.

Q5. Kumamoto Castle's stone walls are known for a shape that curves back more steeply toward the top, making them hard to climb. What is this shape called? (Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts)

  1. Ogi no kobai (fan gradient)
  2. Musha-gaeshi
  3. Shinobi-gaeshi
  4. Ishi-otoshi
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Musha-gaeshi

Musha-gaeshi is a stone wall whose gradient grows steeper toward the top and curves back; its name comes from the idea that even a warrior who climbs it is repelled and turned back.

Q6. What is a castle's entrance called? (Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts)

  1. Kuruwa
  2. Koguchi
  3. Umadashi
  4. Seiro
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Koguchi

A koguchi ("tiger mouth") is a castle entrance. It was often built with a bent passage so that enemies could not go straight through, and was combined with a masugata to firm up the defense.

Q7. What is the central precinct of a castle called? (Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts)

  1. Ni-no-maru
  2. San-no-maru
  3. Honmaru
  4. Nishi-no-maru
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Honmaru

The honmaru is the central enclosure of a castle — the most important precinct, where the main keep and the castle lord's palace were placed.

Q8. Among the forms of main keeps, which is the early form in which a lookout turret is placed on top of a building with an irimoya (hip-and-gable) roof? (Chapter 2: Main Keeps and Turrets)

  1. Soto-gata
  2. Boro-gata
  3. Dokuritsu-shiki
  4. Renritsu-shiki
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Boro-gata

The boro-gata is the older form; the keeps of Himeji Castle and Inuyama Castle are its representative examples. The form that decreases in regular steps is called soto-gata.

Q9. Among the twelve surviving keeps, which combination of castles has main keeps designated as National Treasures? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. Himeji Castle, Matsumoto Castle, Inuyama Castle, Hikone Castle, Matsue Castle
  2. Himeji Castle, Matsumoto Castle, Kumamoto Castle, Nagoya Castle, Osaka Castle
  3. Matsumoto Castle, Inuyama Castle, Hirosaki Castle, Marugame Castle, Kochi Castle
  4. Hikone Castle, Matsue Castle, Uwajima Castle, Maruoka Castle, Bitchu-Matsuyama Castle
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Himeji Castle, Matsumoto Castle, Inuyama Castle, Hikone Castle, Matsue Castle

The National Treasure keeps are those of five castles: Himeji, Matsumoto, Inuyama, Hikone and Matsue. The keeps of Kumamoto Castle and Nagoya Castle are not surviving keeps.

Q10. Which castles still have five-story main keeps? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. Himeji Castle and Matsumoto Castle
  2. Himeji Castle and Kumamoto Castle
  3. Matsumoto Castle and Hikone Castle
  4. Inuyama Castle and Matsue Castle
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Himeji Castle and Matsumoto Castle

Only Himeji Castle and Matsumoto Castle retain five-story main keeps from the Edo period.

Q11. Which castle has a black main keep and is also called the "Crow Castle"? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. Himeji Castle
  2. Matsumoto Castle
  3. Inuyama Castle
  4. Matsuyama Castle
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Matsumoto Castle

Matsumoto Castle's main keep is covered with black lacquered siding boards and bears the nickname Karasu-jo ("Crow Castle").

Q12. Which castle, built by Oda Nobunaga, is regarded as the starting point of the early modern castle with a main keep? (Chapter 1: The Origins and Types of Castles)

  1. Gifu Castle
  2. Kiyosu Castle
  3. Azuchi Castle
  4. Nijo Castle
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Azuchi Castle

Azuchi Castle was built from 1576 and was a groundbreaking castle with a five-story main keep. It burned down shortly after Nobunaga's death.

Q13. Which shogunate law of 1615 ordered the destruction of all castles other than the daimyo's main residence? (Chapter 5: Castle Sieges and the End of Castles)

  1. Castle Abolition Order
  2. One Castle per Province Order
  3. Sword Hunt Order
  4. Laws for the Imperial Court and Courtiers
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. One Castle per Province Order

Under the One Castle per Province Order, in each domain the branch castles and other castles were demolished, leaving a single main castle, and the number of castles decreased greatly.

Q14. Which Meiji government order, issued in 1873, caused many castles to be demolished? (Chapter 5: Castle Sieges and the End of Castles)

  1. Castle Abolition Order
  2. Sword Abolition Order
  3. Land Tax Reform
  4. Dajokan Proclamation
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Castle Abolition Order

Under the Castle Abolition Order, it was decided which castles across the country would survive; those not retained became subject to dismantling or sale.

Q15. Which is the classification of a castle built on flat ground? (Chapter 1: The Origins and Types of Castles)

  1. Mountain castle
  2. Hill castle
  3. Flatland castle
  4. Water castle
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Flatland castle

A flatland castle (hirajiro) is built on level ground and was developed as the center of an Edo-period castle town. Its defenses are supplemented by moats and stone walls.

Q16. Which moat, dug vertically down a mountain slope, prevents enemies from moving sideways across the slope? (Chapter 1: The Origins and Types of Castles)

  1. Yokobori (horizontal moat)
  2. Tatebori (vertical moat)
  3. Mizubori (wet moat)
  4. Karabori (dry moat)
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Tatebori (vertical moat)

A tatebori is a moat dug vertically, perpendicular to the contour lines of a slope, preventing enemies from moving laterally across the slope and working their way around.

Q17. What is the square space set inside a gate, designed so that enemies who have entered can be attacked from all around? (Chapter 3: Stone Walls and Castle Layouts)

  1. Masugata
  2. Umadashi
  3. Ishi-otoshi
  4. Sama
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Masugata

A masugata is a square plaza set inside and outside a gate, structured to halt enemies who have broken in and attack them from all four sides.

Q18. Which daimyo built Kumamoto Castle? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. Kuroda Kanbei
  2. Todo Takatora
  3. Kato Kiyomasa
  4. Kobayakawa Takakage
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. Kato Kiyomasa

Kumamoto Castle was built during the Keicho era by Kato Kiyomasa, known as a master castle builder, and is famed as a nearly impregnable castle.

Q19. Which organization selected the "100 Famous Castles of Japan"? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. Agency for Cultural Affairs
  2. Japan Castle Foundation
  3. Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism
  4. Japan Tourism Association
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Japan Castle Foundation

The 100 Famous Castles of Japan were selected by the Japan Castle Foundation in 2006; together with the Continued 100 Famous Castles of Japan, 200 castles have been selected in total.

Q20. In which year was Himeji Castle registered as a UNESCO World Cultural Heritage site? (Chapter 4: Walking the Famous Castles)

  1. 1973
  2. 1983
  3. 1993
  4. 2003
Answer & explanation

Correct answer: C. 1993

Himeji Castle was registered as a World Cultural Heritage site in 1993, together with Horyu-ji — the first such registrations in Japan.
