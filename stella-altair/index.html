Feeling the Cold in Kyoto — How to Warm Up and What to Review

Hands and feet that are always cold, a chill that seeps in under air conditioning, feet too cold to fall asleep — feeling cold is a concern we hear at every age. Kyoto sits in a basin: winters bring a penetrating cold known locally as sokobie, and summers alternate between hot streets and chilled interiors. This English edition of our Japanese column covers possible factors, easy ways to warm up, points to review in daily life, and when to see a doctor.

What does feeling cold mean?

It differs from person to person: only the hands and feet, a cold abdomen and lower back, or a whole-body chill even in a not-particularly-cold room. Some people feel cold in environments others find comfortable, or chill quickly where air-conditioning blows. When it continues, it can come together with stiff shoulders and back, a sense of leg swelling, or shallow sleep. Start by noticing where, and when, you feel cold.

Possible everyday factors

  • Too little movement — fewer chances for muscles to generate heat and keep circulation moving
  • Long hours in one posture — sitting still can slow circulation in the lower body
  • Air conditioning and light clothing — the neck, wrists, and ankles stay exposed to cold air
  • Shower-only bathing habits — fewer chances to warm the whole body
  • Cold drinks and meals, often
  • Lack of sleep and stress, which can disturb autonomic balance
  • Tight clothing that restricts circulation

  • Review your clothing — avoid tight socks and underwear; layer so you can adjust, and keep something to throw on in air-conditioned places

What does a stiff feeling in the shoulders and neck mean?

"Stiffness" is a general word for a subjective feeling — heaviness, tightness, or a dull ache around the neck, shoulders, and shoulder blades, or a sense that the neck does not turn as freely.   • Too few breaks, too little water — sightseeing can push rest and drinks later into the day

What trouble falling asleep and lingering fatigue mean

Falling asleep is not only about how tired the body is. Alongside physical tiredness, the mind settling down, body temperature easing off, and the quietness of the room all play a part. After a day full of new sights and movement, the body can be tired while the mind stays switched on — so some people find that the more tired they are, the longer sleep takes to come. Fatigue also varies in kind: heaviness in the legs from walking, stiffness in the shoulders and back, or a general feeling of not having rested even after sleeping. Start by noticing which pattern fits your night: taking long to fall asleep, waking during the night, or waking unrefreshed.

Possible factors after a day of sightseeing

  • Excitement and a full head — memories of the day, photos to sort, and planning tomorrow can keep the mind busy after lights-out
  • An unfamiliar sleeping environment — a different pillow, sounds, light, and room temperature than at home
  • Walking more than usual — leg fatigue and muscle tension can make it hard to settle
  • Screens late into the evening — checking routes and photos on a phone until just before bed
  • Caffeine and alcohol — coffee or tea late in the day, and alcohol in the evening, which some people find makes sleep shallower
  • Bath timing and body temperature — going to bed still warmed through, or chilled from air conditioning, can affect how sleep comes
  • A shifted daily rhythm — early starts, irregular meals, and accumulated tiredness from the days before the trip

  • Soak in the bath a little before bed — lukewarm water, at a temperature that feels good, rather than very hot; leave some time between the bath and bed
  • Ease the legs gently — after a long day of walking, gentle calf stretches and slow ankle circles, within a pain-free range
  • Dim the room and put the phone down earlier — finish checking tomorrow's route a little sooner, and spend the last part of the evening quietly
  • Adjust drinks in the evening — if caffeine late in the day concerns you, warm caffeine-free drinks are one option some people choose
  • Keep the room comfortable — adjust bedding and air conditioning so you are neither hot nor chilled, and keep your feet from getting cold
  • Do not force sleep — if sleep does not come, rather than straining in bed, some people get up briefly, read in low light, and return when sleepy
  • Take the next morning gently — when fatigue lingers, leave margin in the day's walking distance and schedule rather than packing the day

These are general ideas for spending the evening, not a guarantee of any effect. If a particular approach does not suit how you feel, do not force it.

  • Trouble sleeping continues for weeks after you are back home, not only while travelling
  • Daytime sleepiness interferes with work, driving, or daily life
  • Your snoring is loud, or you have been told your breathing stops during sleep
  • You wake many times in the night, or wake very early and cannot return to sleep, and this keeps happening
  • Fatigue comes with fever, weight change, palpitations, or a low mood that continues
  • Legs feel restless or uncomfortable at night and keep you from sleeping

Column｜Seasonal Change & Heaviness

Seasonal Heaviness of Body and Mind in Kyoto — Living with Stress and a Sense of Autonomic Imbalance

As the seasons turn, you may wake to a heavy body, feel vaguely unrefreshed through the day, and notice your mood turning grey as well. Kyoto sits in a basin where the gap between morning and midday temperatures can be wide, so the change of season is something the body registers keenly here. This English edition of our Japanese column gives a general explanation of the heaviness many people feel at the turn of the seasons, organises possible factors without overstating them, and covers gentle self-care and the signs that mean you should see a doctor.

What this seasonal heaviness is

The heaviness felt at the turn of the seasons is not one single symptom. For some it is physical — a heavy body on waking, tiredness that lingers, stiff shoulders, a dull head. For others it is more in the feelings — low motivation, broken sleep, a mind that will not settle. The balance between activity and rest in the body is often described as being kept by the autonomic nervous system, and when the environment changes quickly, some people feel that balance is harder to keep. Start by noticing your own pattern: is the heaviness mainly in the body, in sleep, or in your mood?

Possible factors at the turn of the seasons

  • Wide temperature swings within a day — in a basin like Kyoto, mornings and evenings can be much cooler than midday, and dressing and room temperature are hard to keep in step
  • Changes in air pressure and weather — unsettled spells around seasonal fronts, when some people feel heaviness or a dull head
  • Shifting daylight and daily rhythm — sunrise and sunset times move, and the rhythm of waking and sleeping can drift with them
  • New-season busyness and stress — changes at work or at home often cluster at this time of year, and tiredness can build without being noticed
  • Heating and cooling — moving between warmed or cooled rooms and the outdoors, and dry indoor air
  • Accumulated tiredness and broken sleep — nights of shallow sleep carrying over into daytime heaviness

In traditional East Asian medicine, heaviness at the change of season is often related to the balance of the Spleen, which is linked with fluid metabolism, and the Liver, which is linked with the smooth flow of qi. See our Guide by Concern on the home page under swelling and heavy legs, and whole-body fatigue.

Gentle self-care for the season change

  • Dress in layers you can adjust — a cardigan or scarf you can add or remove as the temperature shifts through the day
  • Keep wake and sleep times steady — even on days off, avoid large shifts, and get morning light when you wake
  • Warm the body gently — a lukewarm bath rather than a very hot one, and warmth at the neck, abdomen, and ankles when you feel chilled
  • Move a little, often — a short walk or gentle stretching, within a comfortable range, rather than leaving the body still all day
  • Eat and drink regularly — try not to skip meals, and take warm drinks and water through the day
  • Make time to switch off — even briefly, time away from screens, in a scent or music you like, at a pace that suits you
  • Leave margin in your schedule — in weeks when the heaviness stands out, avoid packing sightseeing or work too tightly

These are general ideas for daily life, not a guarantee of any effect. If a particular approach does not suit how you feel, do not force it.

See a doctor if…

  • The heaviness or fatigue continues for weeks regardless of the season, or is getting stronger
  • A low mood, loss of appetite, or loss of interest in things you usually enjoy continues
  • Daytime sleepiness interferes with work, driving, or daily life
  • The heaviness comes with fever, weight change, palpitations, or breathlessness
  • Headaches are frequent, severe, or different from usual
  • You are taking regular medication and feel a clear change in your condition

季節の変わり目の重だるさとは

季節の変わり目に感じる重だるさは、ひとつの決まった症状を指す言葉ではありません。 朝起きたときに体が重い、昼間も疲れが抜けない、肩や背中が張る、頭がぼんやりするといった体の重さとして感じる方もいれば、 やる気が出にくい、眠りが浅い、気持ちが落ち着かないといった心の面の変化として感じる方もいます。

季節の変わり目に重だるさを感じるとき、その背景には一つの原因ではなく、気候と生活の変化がいくつか重なっていることがあります。一般に、要因として次のようなものが挙げられます。いずれも断定ではなく、ご自身の過ごし方を振り返るための整理としてご覧ください。

  • 一日の気温差の大きさ：盆地の京都では、朝晩と日中の気温差が大きい日があります。服装や室温の調整が追いつかない日が続くと、体への負担を感じる方もいます
  • 気圧や天気の変化：季節の境目で天気が不安定な時期に、体の重さや頭の重さを感じる方もいます
  • 日の長さと生活リズムの変化：日の出・日の入りの時刻が移り変わる時期は、起床や就寝のリズムもずれやすくなります
  • 新年度・新生活の忙しさとストレス：仕事や家庭の節目が重なりやすい時期で、気づかないうちに疲れが積み重なっていることもあります
  • 冷暖房と乾燥：冷暖房の効いた室内と外との行き来や、室内の乾燥が続く環境も、体調の感じ方に関わることがあります
  • 疲れの蓄積と眠りの浅さ：眠りの浅い夜が重なると、翌日の重だるさとして持ち越しやすくなります
